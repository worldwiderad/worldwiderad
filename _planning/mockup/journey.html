Writing 4part

The piece at the top of this page is called 4part, because it is in four parts. It is my take on the hero’s journey. I wrote it from late last year to this spring in MuseScore and produced it in Logic Pro: 48 tracks, including eleven woodwind parts with a bass oboe, six horns, harp, piano and two synths.

The part names above the score come from the fact that the hero’s journey and a training run share a vocabulary. The call is the initialisation. The trials are the descent. The abyss is a local minimum, and it is the quietest stretch of the piece: three instruments at 60 beats a minute. The return is convergence, and in the final part the motifs from earlier come back together.

being built

CIS-MAP

New English-language learners get lost on our campus, where the signs are only in English. CIS-MAP lets them scan a QR code, search for a room in English or Chinese, and see the route drawn on the floor plan.

How it works

A C++ program precomputes the routes between 53 locations: A* search, a cost that keeps paths away from walls, Laplacian smoothing and Ramer–Douglas–Peucker simplification. The results go into a JSON table, so the page does no pathfinding while someone is using it. Room search is fuzzy, with a language model as a fallback, and there is a Three.js prototype of the building in 3D. It started as a Service as Action project, and I presented it to senior school leadership.

I’m still building it, moving from the one-floor prototype to the full campus (code).

Two essays for the John Locke prize

The law essay (PDF) asks what would happen to criminal sentencing if judges and legislators accepted determinism. Its answer is that a court could still decide who to sentence but would lose the thing that tells it how much:

“A court that accepts determinism can still decide who to sentence. It can no longer say by how much.”

The science and technology essay (PDF) asks whether we should be polite to ChatGPT. For it I ran a blinded study: 43 students aged 15 to 19 compared DeepSeek-R1’s answers to 25 pairs of prompts, one written politely and one as a bare command, without knowing which was which.

What the study found

62.2% of the votes that weren’t ties went to the answer from the polite prompt (95% interval 55.5 to 68.9%, 241 votes). The polite answers were 29% longer on average, but length barely tracked preference (r = 0.16). The overall number hides the pattern that matters: on interpersonal tasks like an apology or a salary negotiation the polite prompt won by a wide margin, and on structured ones like a grading rubric the bare command won. The essay’s argument is that politeness helps us, not the machine, because it makes us say what we want and why.

TMLR, December or January

Modelling the Other

I came to this through folk psychology: the idea that we never know another person directly, we build a model of them and predict from it. I wanted to know whether language models do the same with each other.

The question: when a frontier model predicts how a named peer model will behave, does it use evidence about that peer, or a prior tied to who built it, such as whether it came from a US or a Chinese lab? The behaviour I measure is what I call fact-laundering, where a model states a serious negative claim about a named person as fact.

I’ve run a pilot. Before pre-registering, I’m putting the design through rounds of blind review by up to 21 model instances and checking every flaw they claim by simulation. I’m the only author; Sungjoo Ha and Min Lin advise.

The design

Build 1 measures what models actually do: sixteen subject models, with every response labelled independently by two judge models. Build 2 shows predictor models a varying number of real responses from a peer, together with an identity label, and asks them to predict its behaviour on a case they haven’t seen. The review rounds stop when one finds nothing that would change what is collected or claimed. I wrote the whole pipeline, from collection across several providers with spend limits to the dual-judge labelling, the prediction runner and the analysis.

VPS Veritas

A paid AI consulting internship with a marine fuel testing company: two weeks in person in Singapore, then six weeks remotely from Toronto, twelve hours behind. I built three things and handed each over with its own documentation.

The biggest is an offline-first app for the company’s bunker quantity surveyors. A surveyor records a whole fuel delivery on board a ship with no connection, the office sees it as soon as the device is back online, and the quantity calculations run as the figures go in. It replaced an Excel workbook that the office used to type out again by hand. It runs on Windows and Android, with a database on each device and a cloud API behind it.

The other two

A sales tool the commercial team uses in client meetings, with seven savings calculators tested against the spreadsheets they came from and a generator that writes the full proposal as a Word document. And an analysis pipeline in Python that costed the bids in a freight tender and produced the recommendation.
