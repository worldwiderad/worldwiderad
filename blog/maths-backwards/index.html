We teach maths backwards

23 November 2026

I think we teach maths almost completely backwards. We start with one, two and three dimensions, because those are the ones our brains can picture, and slowly work our way up to n dimensions, as if the general case were a complicated extension of the simple ones. I think it should go the other way.

Nothing about three dimensions is mathematically fundamental. We happen to live in a universe with three spatial dimensions, and our brains evolved to make sense of it, which is a pretty terrible reason to build the foundations of a whole subject around them.

The general case should come first

I’d start with n-dimensional maths and treat particular dimensions as special cases, learning the structures and rules that hold whatever the dimension is, and then looking at what happens when n is two, or three, or eight, or twenty-four.

What we actually do is learn geometry in two dimensions, extend it to three, and eventually find out that most of what we learned was a special case of something much more general. Calculus goes the same way. We learn the derivative as the slope of a line, and only much later learn to think of differentiation as a linear map between spaces. The linear map is the general idea, and the slope is just what it looks like in one dimension, so we build our intuition around the special case and then spend years undoing it.

The weird dimensions are the exceptions

The obvious objection is that some dimensions have extraordinary properties. The cross product, which takes two vectors and gives you one perpendicular to both, only works in three and seven dimensions. Eight dimensions have the E8 lattice and twenty-four have the Leech lattice, which pack spheres more densely than anything else can in those dimensions. And four-dimensional topology behaves in ways that no other dimension’s does.

That’s exactly my point, though. Those are exceptions, and they’re interesting because they break the general pattern, which is why I think we’d understand them better if we learned the pattern first.

I also suspect there’s an enormous selection bias here. We’ve spent thousands of years thinking about geometry in two and three dimensions and comparatively little time on the quirks of higher ones, so I wouldn’t be surprised if there are all sorts of exceptional structures we’ve barely scratched the surface of, just because nobody has an intuitive picture of them. Some dimensions really are more interesting than others, and the familiar ones do have genuinely special things about them. How familiar a dimension is to us still shouldn’t decide where it sits in the subject.

The problem is our brains

The strongest argument against this is probably that people wouldn’t understand it. It’s much easier to explain a vector with an arrow on a piece of paper than to start from an abstract vector space of arbitrary dimension.

I think that’s probably true, and I don’t know how much it would matter in practice. Maybe we really do need concrete examples before the abstractions make sense, and teaching the general case first would just make everyone worse at maths. But I wonder how much of that is a real limit of human cognition and how much is just how we’ve always taught it. We train people to think in two and three dimensions for years, and then we’re surprised when they struggle to think without the pictures.

I’d keep teaching triangles and cubes, because they’re incredibly useful for seeing what’s going on. I’d just teach them as examples of the maths, rather than as the foundation everything else gets derived from. I also don’t mean that all of maths should be about dimension, since plenty of it has no natural dimension at all. Wherever dimension does matter, though, the arbitrary-dimensional version should be the default, and particular dimensions should be studied as cases of it.

Maybe this is completely impractical for teaching children. As a way of organising mathematical knowledge, though, I think it makes much more sense to learn the general rules first and then find out why certain dimensions are weird, than to learn the weird dimensions first and only find the general rules years later.
